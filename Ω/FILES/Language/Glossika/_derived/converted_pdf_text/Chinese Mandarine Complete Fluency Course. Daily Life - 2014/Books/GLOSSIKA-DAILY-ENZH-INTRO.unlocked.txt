A transformation is restating the same sentence with the same

●What are substitution drills?
Substitution drills are more or less the opposite of transformation 
drills. Instead of restating the same thing in a different way, 
you're saying a different thing using the exact same way. So using 
the example from above we can create this substitution drill: 
- Could someone help me with my bags?
- Could someone help me with making dinner?
(We have replaced the noun with a gerund phrase or verbal-noun 
phrase). The sentence has a different meaning but it's using the 
same structure. What's great about this substitution drill is that 
we've actually altered the grammar from noun to gerund, so the 
learner is getting practice with not just vocabulary substitution but 
phrase substitution as well. In English and other European languages 
this is hard because learners without experience really don't know 
whether -ing should be added to the word in this position or not. 
And by doing several dozen sentences with similar structures, that 
-ing becomes quite natural for the learner to the point where he/
she doesn't need to think about it.

Tones
T
here doesn't really have to be anything mysterious 
about Chinese tones, or the tones of any language 
for that matter. In fact, "intonation" is a kind of tone, 
and there really is no atonal language. All languages 
have different pitches that occur in their words. In fact 
the change and variation of tone in a single language 
from generation to generation, century after century, 
actually go through a process. The author, Mike 
Campbell, even postulates that this process is cyclical 
and in the range of several thousand years.

59
f
You
Tube
C
hinese and Tibetan (and Burmese) all sprung from 
the same source thousands of years ago. But they 
were not tonal languages. Chinese developed tones 
earlier than the other others, Tibetan developing tones 
only in the last few centuries. The tones of Chinese are 
recorded in ancient texts, and although Tibetan has 
an ancient writing system, the tones are not recorded 
there. Most Tibetan languages have lost almost all of 
their consonant clusters which has caused tonogenesis. 
There are always those traditional varieties though, 
a western Tibetan language spoken in India called 
Ladakhi still has all the consonant clusters and is 
toneless.

60
f
You

T
he Chinese languages themselves are on different 
paths of tone development. Mandarin is becoming 
more and more like English, due to compounding 
words into two or three syllables and tones becoming 
weaker and weaker. In fact, Mandarin is one of the only 
Chinese languages to have a neutral tone, just like an 
English unstressed syllable.

61
f
You
Tube
T
he Min languages, Wu, and Jin all have developed 
very complex tone sandhi (mild to extreme 
respectively), which means that each syllable changes 
in relation to the syllable next to it. Min languages 
change all tones if they occur before another tone 
which is similar to the 3rd tone change in Mandarin, 
with about 8-10 sandhi. Wu changes all the tones in a 
particular word, depending on the voicing quality of the 
onset consonants, for a total of 90 variations. Jin goes 
to the extent that it determines tone changes based on 
grammatical function and the particular tones before 
and after, thus producing more than 300 variations. 
Many scholars have noted that these languages, Wu 
in particular, are moving towards pitch-accent which is 
found in Japanese and to a lesser extent in Korean.
